Methodology

The Global Warming Index attribution method estimates contributions to observed warming by separating out the contributions from various human-induced and natural forcing drivers of climate change, and natural year-to-year fluctuations known as internal variability.

The attribution method separates the warming contributions through a series of simple steps:

  1. Aggregate emissions and effective radiative forcings data: Collect latest updated timeseries for all climate drivers (forcings), including all natural and anthropogenic sources [IGCC 2025, following IPCC AR6 WGI Ch.7 method] . The individual forcings are aggregated into three groups:
    • Natural forcings forcings: solar and volcanic activity
    • Greenhouse Gas forcings forcings: CO2, CH4, N2O, and F-gases
    • Other human forcings: aerosol radiation interaction, aerosol-cloud interaction, black carbon on snow, contrails, ozone, stratospheric H2O, and land use change
  2. Estimate temperature response using simple model: A simple climate model that is commonly used in the IPCC [FaIR v2.0 (Leach et al., 2021)] is used to convert the forcing timeseries into initial estimates of the temperature response for each component. Warming is defined relative to the standard 1850-1900 baseline, but but can be simply re-calculated for any baseline as needed.
  3. Combine components: Sums are performed to combine the different warming contributions:
    • Human-induced = greenhouse gas + other human forcings
    • Total forced warming = human-induced + natural
  4. Compare total forced warming to observations: The estimated total forced warming is compared to observed global mean surface temperature data.
    • Residual = observed – total forced warming
  5. Scale forced warming to best match observations: A simple least-squares fit is performed between the estimated total forced warming and:
    1. realised global mean surface temperature from observations, and
    2. alternative possible internal variability realisations from models
    These additional constraints are used to scale each of the estimated forced warming components, improving their accuracy and precision. This process adjusts the warming estimates for all components in order optimise the fit between modelled and real-world warming. Note: the optimisation removes any spurious contribution from forced warming from the residual, so we interpret the optimised residual as the internal variability.
  6. Extract Global Warming Index: The resulting index shows the best estimate of human-induced warming along with the 5-95% uncertainty range due to the combined uncertainties from forcings, observations, climate response and internal variability.

Uncertainty: Uncertainty is estimated by repeating the above calculations for a very large Monte Carlo ensemble of possible variations of: (i) forcings ensemble (1000 members of ERF ), (ii) observational data (200 members of HadcRUT5 ), (iii) climate response parameters (~30 members of 6 FaIR emulation parameters of CMIP6 simluations) and (iv) alternative internal variability realisations (200 members of CMIP6 pre-industrial control simulations).

Background to the Global Warming Index

The globalwarmingindex.org website shows an up-to-the-second index of human-induced warming relative to the second half of the 19th century (1850-1900).

The Global Warming Index was first introduced in Otto et al. (2015) based on a "detection and attribution" methodology originally developed by Hasselmann (1997). It was published with a comprehensive uncertainty analysis in Haustein et al. (2017), and work extending the robustness and utility of the Index is ongoing.

The Global Warming Index is been used extensively in the IPCC Assessment Reports to assess the level of global warming (including the Special Report on 1.5°C in 2018 , and the 6th Assessment Report in 2021 ), and for more broadly tracking progression towards the Paris Agreement goals.

Annual Assessments and Reach

The GWI is under active scientific development and maintenance by researchers from the Environmental Change Institute at the University of Oxford, UK, who provide systematic operarational annual updates of the data and methodology.

These latest GWI datasets are peer reviewed and published as part of the Indicators of Global Climate Change (IGCC) initiative global annual climate assessments, which are released for policymakers at the yearly UNFCCC Bonn meetings. These assessments have additionally been presented at the annual UNFCCC COP conferences, and included in the WMO State of the Climate Report .

The GWI is now widely used by policymakers, scientists, and climate communicators around the world to track progress towards international climate goals. As one example of reach, the IGCC's 2024 assessment was the most featured climate article in the media in 2025, according to Carbon Brief .
